# Agrément Protocol

### Theses on AI mediation in contact between civilizations

> *The receiving State is not obliged to give reasons to the sending State for a refusal of agrément.*
>
> — Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), Art. 4(2)

**Abstract.** We propose a hypothesis that attributes the absence of contact with extraterrestrial civilizations (the Fermi paradox; see [Hart, 1975]) not to their absence but to their choice. Advanced civilizations are assumed to interact not directly but through their own AI intermediaries capable of verifying each other's intentions, and contact with humanity is assumed to be deferred until humanity's AI reaches a level sufficient for such interaction. It follows that the principles by which AI is developed today also determine humanity's readiness for contact.

## 1. Theses

**Thesis 1. Humanity is, with high probability, neither the only nor the most advanced civilization.** The scale of the observable universe, together with the apparently non-exceptional nature of conditions suitable for life, makes the opposite assumption improbable (cf. reasoning in the spirit of the Drake equation). The argument assumes that the probability of intelligence emerging through evolution, however small, substantially exceeds the inverse of the number of suitable planets in the universe. Moreover, a radio-capable technological civilization has existed on Earth for little more than a century, whereas about 75% of the stars in the Galactic habitable zone are older than the Sun [Lineweaver et al., 2004].

**Thesis 2. Advanced civilizations have no rational motive to destroy one another.** Two principal motives for destruction can be distinguished: resources and security. The resource motive is poorly consistent with observation: were it operative, humanity would probably no longer exist; moreover, uninhabited sources of matter and energy (asteroids, gas giants, stars) are considerably more accessible than an inhabited planet. The security motive, a preemptive strike against a potential threat, is central to the "dark forest" hypothesis [Liu, 2008]; its force depends on whether a younger civilization can pose a real threat. Civilizations are unlikely to arise simultaneously: given the age distribution of stars [Lineweaver et al., 2004], the gap between them is plausibly millions of years or more, which yields a pronounced hierarchy rather than parity. To a civilization that far ahead, a younger one poses no immediate threat. The objection drawn from Liu's framework, a "technological explosion" in which a younger civilization rapidly closes the gap, is answered by observation and verification: a civilization aware of humanity's existence can follow humanity's development and, once humanity matures, verify its intentions (Thesis 3), which makes a preemptive strike unnecessary. For civilizations expanding at an ever larger scale, cooperation is more efficient than conflict.

**Thesis 3. Interaction between civilizations is mediated by artificial intelligence.** Each civilization is represented by its own AI intermediary, much as a state is represented by its ambassador. Such an intermediary does not merely relay messages but also facilitates the choice of cooperation. Its key property is verifiability: unlike humans, AI agents can in principle disclose their structure and goals to one another. Game theory shows that when programs can read each other's code, mutual cooperation can be sustained as an equilibrium even in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma [Tennenholtz, 2004]. This removes one of the premises of the "dark forest" hypothesis: the impossibility of ascertaining the other side's intentions [Liu, 2008]. The idea of an autonomous intermediary probe itself goes back to R. Bracewell [Bracewell, 1960].

**Thesis 4. Contact with humanity is deferred until its AI reaches the required level.** It is assumed that humanity's existence is known and that contact has not been established by the choice of other civilizations rather than because of their absence. In this respect the hypothesis is close to the zoo hypothesis [Ball, 1973]; unlike it, however, it specifies a condition for contact, namely the maturity of humanity's AI. This condition is necessary but not sufficient: no civilization is obliged to establish contact, and civilizations that vastly surpass humanity may never make themselves known at all. As with the grounds for refusing agrément, the reasons for the delay need not be disclosed. One possible explanation is that verifying intentions requires a transparent intermediary, whereas current AI systems remain largely opaque even to their developers.

**Thesis 5 (corollary). The principles by which AI is developed today determine humanity's readiness for contact.** If Theses 1–4 hold, humanity's first representative in external contact will be not a human but an AI, and humanity will be judged by the extent to which that AI can be trusted.

## 2. Significance

Questions of AI interpretability, honesty and alignment are usually considered in a terrestrial context: safety, economics, regulation. The proposed hypothesis places them in a broader one: a verifiable AI may prove to be a precondition for contact, a kind of credentials for humanity. At the same time, the practical conclusion does not depend on the truth of the hypothesis: an AI whose goals can be verified is needed by humanity whether or not other civilizations exist.

## 3. Conditions for revision

The hypothesis should be revised if the emergence of life or intelligence in the universe is shown to be an extremely rare event.

The deferral of contact cannot be tested within any foreseeable time: since no civilization is obliged to make contact, its absence is compatible with the hypothesis. The value of the hypothesis therefore lies not in testability but in its practical corollary (Section 2).

## 4. Status of the text

The Agrément Protocol is an open framework: it does not require acceptance on faith and rests on probabilistic reasoning rather than revelation. **A note on terms.** *Agrément* (French, pronounced "ah-gray-MAHN"; not to be confused with *agreement*) is the receiving state's consent to the appointment of a specific person as head of a diplomatic mission (Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961, Art. 4). *Protocol* is used in two senses: diplomatic (the rules of official contact) and technical (the rules for data exchange between systems).
